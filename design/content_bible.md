# Pack the Keep — Content Bible

## Content direction

**Pack the Keep** is a defense game about choosing a doctrine and making it visible in space. Packs are not collectible clutter and commanders are not cosmetic skins. A pack should change what the player builds. A commander should change what the player notices. The keep should feel like a place assembled under pressure, not a menu of percentage bonuses.

The campaign follows one neglected fort through a single season. Nearby households need shelter, the keep’s rooms have different practical histories, and the Ashen Host is approaching with several ways to make a static defense fail. The player’s job is not merely to survive waves. It is to decide what the keep is for: a compact fortress, a mobile refuge, or a structure that buys time for evacuation.

The content system has four jobs. It must make pack choices emotionally and mechanically legible, make each room matter before it becomes a target, turn enemy doctrines into readable questions, and give partial breach a meaningful narrative role. A damaged keep should tell the player what was missing and offer a shorter, stronger next decision.

> **Content rule:** Every pack, commander, room, or event must change the player’s spatial question. If the player can choose it without changing where defenders stand, what they protect, or how they recover, it is probably flavor rather than content.

## Tone and voice

The tone is lively, practical, and intimate. This is a fort full of people who repair things, argue about space, and know which door sticks in cold weather. The game can have humor, but it should not become a toy-box parody. The threat is real because the keep contains ordinary lives and finite materials.

The writing should use physical language: braces, lanes, hinges, signal, sightline, reserve, breach, shelter, bell, wall, and empty space. Avoid explaining a mechanic only through percentages. “The scout post lets you see the climbers before they reach the roofline” is better than “visibility +15%.”

The Ashen Host is not a single villain. It is an invasion composed of doctrines that answer different weaknesses. Raiders want the gate to become the whole battle. Sappers want support rooms to fail. Climbers want walls to create false confidence. Siege beasts want the player to discover that a strong room can still damage everything around it.

## Campaign premise

The keep was built as a regional watch station and then neglected when the old road shifted south. Its outer wall remains, but the rooms have been repurposed: the armory stores empty racks, the workshop holds broken braces, the chapel is full of blankets, and the supply room has more labels than supplies.

The player receives one season of authority to turn the keep into a refuge before the Ashen Host arrives. Two commanders offer competing doctrines. The Castellan believes a keep survives through adjacency, layers, and disciplined compactness. The Warden believes it survives through movement, signals, and timely response. Both are viable, but neither is universally correct.

The campaign’s pressure escalates through three waves. The first teaches a single doctrine. The second combines a front attack with a support-room threat. The third introduces area pressure and forces the player to decide whether to defend the structure, preserve a refuge, or use the keep to buy time for withdrawal.

## Campaign structure

The four chapters should support a 30–45 minute first playthrough and a 15–25 minute replay. They should be presented as a sequence of short reports, commander conversations, pack reveals, and wave outcomes rather than long cutscenes.

| Chapter | Player experience | Required content beats | Systemic proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Open the Armory** | Choose a doctrine and build a readable first layout. | Commander choice, first pack, room inspection, first forecast. | The player can describe what the chosen pack is for and where it belongs. |
| **2. The First Bell** | Learn one invasion doctrine and intervene without frantic execution. | Gate-assault forecast, first wave, pause-and-read moment, first repair. | A prepared defense answers the expected threat, but the layout is not perfect. |
| **3. The Wrong Wall** | Experience a partial breach and rebuild around a lesson. | Second pack choice, distributed sabotage, support-room failure, repair phase. | A breach changes the next layout while preserving a meaningful recovery path. |
| **4. The Refuge Bell** | Decide whether the keep is a fortress, refuge, or delay mechanism. | Siege-beast warning, final pack, last intervention, keep report. | The outcome explains who was protected and which spatial doctrine prevailed. |

### Opening sequence: two keys

The player enters the keep with two keys and two offers. The Castellan points out that the armory, workshop, and barracks are close enough to support one another if the player leaves room for connections. The Warden points out that the outer wall is already compromised and asks where a mobile reserve could move without crossing the entire keep.

The first pack choice should be a meaningful statement, not a randomized obstacle. Pike Line teaches the value of narrow corridors. Field Engineers teach the value of redundancy. Firekeepers teach the value of denial zones. Scouts teach the value of information. The player may open one pack before the first wave and earn a second after seeing the first forecast.

## Rooms and spaces

The keep has a compact room graph. Each room should have a clear purpose, a visual motif, and a threat that makes the player think differently about its placement or protection. The two expandable spaces should create campaign identity rather than become mandatory late-game upgrades.

| Room | Dramatic identity | What the player wants | What the room wants from the layout |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Outer Wall** | The visible promise of time. | Braces, clear lines, and a fallback. | Support that can reach it before damage becomes a breach. |
| **Inner Yard** | Flexible space for movement and response. | Open lanes and a reserve position. | Not being filled so tightly that a bypass becomes a trap. |
| **Gate** | The obvious pressure point. | Concentrated defense and a clear retreat line. | A doctrine that does not make every defense depend on it. |
| **Armory** | Where pack choices become physical. | Space to stage pieces and change doctrine. | Protection from sappers and room for future choices. |
| **Workshop** | The keep’s second chance. | Materials, repair access, and a visible work lane. | Redundancy so a single hit does not remove recovery. |
| **Barracks** | Where defenders remain people. | Short routes to threatened spaces and safe rest. | Clear assignments and room to reposition. |
| **Supply Room** | The dependency behind every intervention. | Protection, labels, and reserve materials. | Not becoming the only place the rest of the keep can function. |
| **North Tower** | Future sight and warning. | A signal line and a reason to invest in information. | Protection from climbers and a useful view of the approach. |
| **Old Chapel** | A contested refuge space. | Blankets, morale, and an evacuation route. | A layout that accepts the keep may be shelter before it is victory. |

Room damage should be visible in stages: strained, damaged, breached, and stabilized. The player should see which function is lost at each stage. A damaged workshop should reduce repair availability; a damaged supply room should reduce reserve; a breached gate should change the threat path; a damaged chapel should reduce refuge capacity.

## Commanders

### The Castellan — a keep is a sentence

The Castellan is a keeper of old plans who believes a defense is strongest when each room finishes the sentence begun by the room beside it. Their passive makes adjacent rooms reinforce one another. Their ability, Lockdown, temporarily restores condition across placed pieces.

The Castellan’s strength is compact layered defense. Their drawback is slow repositioning. The player must decide early which spaces are worth making strong and which can be sacrificed. The Castellan should speak in measured structural metaphors, but their arc must reveal that compactness can become a trap when the player leaves no route for people to move.

The Castellan’s final question is “Did we make a fortress, or did we make a box?” The strongest ending for this commander is an intact keep with a clear interior reserve, not a wall of overlapping pieces.

### The Warden — movement is a wall

The Warden is a field commander who has spent more time escorting people between defenses than standing behind one. Their passive improves defender repositioning. Their ability, Rally, restores morale and creates a short burst of coordinated response.

The Warden’s strength is adaptation. Their drawback is a lower starting materials reserve and greater dependence on movement lanes. The player must leave space intentionally and protect signals. The Warden should speak plainly, interrupt plans when a route is blocked, and learn that movement without information becomes panic.

The Warden’s final question is “Who can still move when the bell changes?” The strongest ending for this commander preserves refuge capacity and civilian movement even if the keep is scarred.

## Packs

Each pack contains two physical pieces and one doctrine statement. The pack reveal should show the pieces in the keep before the player commits. The player must understand purpose, footprint, limitation, and the problem the pack solves.

| Pack | Doctrine | Immediate question | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pike Line** | Compact corridors | Where can a narrow approach become strong enough to hold? | Weak when enemies spread through open yard. |
| **Field Engineers** | Redundancy | Which support system will make the next mistake survivable? | Consumes materials and creates a valuable target. |
| **Firekeepers** | Denial zones | Which open space should become dangerous to cross? | Needs sightlines and can restrict friendly movement. |
| **Scouts** | Early warning | Where will information buy enough time to matter? | Information requires a response space and available command points. |

Packs should be offered in small choices rather than a large inventory. A second-wave reward can show three packs with one reserve slot. A redraw or scout preview is acceptable, but the player should never receive a draw with no viable response to the forecast.

## Enemy doctrines

The enemy list remains compact while doctrines provide variety. The first wave should teach gate assault with Raiders. The second wave should combine Sappers with Raiders or Climbers. The third should use a Siege Beast to test whether the player has preserved space and recovery.

**Gate assault** asks whether the player can concentrate enough strength at the obvious entrance without making the entire keep depend on one gate. **Distributed sabotage** asks whether support rooms and recovery systems are protected. **Feint and flank** asks whether the player has an interior response and usable movement lanes. **Area pressure** asks whether the player can accept a damaged perimeter without losing the refuge function.

Every doctrine must be visible in the forecast. The player should see a gate marker, support-room warning, wall bypass marker, or area-impact radius before the wave begins. No doctrine should have one mandatory counter. A player can respond through pack choice, commander ability, spatial layout, timing, or controlled sacrifice.

## Event writing rules

Events are short preparation or recovery scenes tied to a room, pack, commander, or wave condition. They must produce a build decision, a changed layout, a resource tradeoff, or a meaningful report. Avoid flavor-only pack descriptions once the campaign begins.

The four vertical-slice events are:

| Event | Initial lesson | Escalation | Content consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Two Keys, One Keep** | Commanders change what a viable layout looks like. | The chosen doctrine creates a different weakness. | Commander, starting resources, and first layout question. |
| **The Bell Has a Pattern** | A forecast is useful when the player can act on it. | A gate threat tests whether the keep has a reserve. | Gate concentration, movement, signals, and wave timing. |
| **The Wrong Wall** | A partial breach should reveal a support failure. | Distributed sabotage makes the wrong room important. | Repair, sealing, counterattack, or compact retreat. |
| **Something Large at the Tree Line** | The final threat tests the keep’s purpose, not only its health. | Area pressure makes empty space and refuge capacity matter. | Hold, scarred refuge, evacuation, or recoverable collapse. |

The event system should support variants based on commander, owned packs, room condition, and breach level. The event text can change from “the gate is under pressure” to “the workshop is the target,” but the consequence categories should remain stable for testing.

## Progression

Progression has two tracks: **Fortification Doctrine** and **Keep Recovery**. The first changes how space and adjacent rooms behave. The second makes partial breach survivable and creates alternatives to rebuilding the same opening.

The first milestone declares the player’s doctrine through commander and pack selection. The second rewards the player for reading the first wave and using pause or intervention. The third proves that a damaged keep can recover. The fourth rewards a coherent two-pack doctrine rather than a pile of unrelated pieces. The final milestone opens the Refuge Bell decision.

A returning player should be able to use the Castellan with Pike Line and Field Engineers for a compact fortress, or the Warden with Scouts and Firekeepers for a mobile denial layout. Those should feel like different games within the same keep without requiring a huge content library.

## Endings

**Stone With a Memory** is the intact-hold ending. The keep survives because its layout expressed a clear doctrine and preserved enough reserve. The report should name the room or connection that made the defense work so the player learns from success.

**The Open Yard** is the scarred-refuge ending. The keep loses condition but preserves movement, civilian safety, and enough rooms to remain useful. The player wins by leaving space and accepting that a perfect perimeter is not the only objective.

**The Bell Carries** is the evacuation ending. The player opens the Old Chapel or another refuge route and moves people and essential packs to a second position. The keep is partly lost, but the campaign turns the loss into a new defensive beginning.

**A Lesson in Empty Space** is the collapse ending. The keep fails as a defensive position, but the report identifies the missing recovery option or spatial assumption and unlocks a shorter retry. The game should never imply that the failure was inevitable or that the player needed a single secret build order.

## Agent implementation guidance

Use the stable IDs in `content/content_manifest.json`. Display pack content as name, purpose, footprint, limitation, and choice text. Display each room as purpose, condition, threat, and available actions. Convert event effects into explicit simulation commands; never parse narrative prose to mutate state.

The first content slice should show commander choice, one pack preview, one room inspection, and one wave forecast. The second should connect pack and commander definitions to placement and invasion rules. The third should validate references, pack piece membership, commander viability, doctrine coverage, and ending reachability. Narrative text should remain short enough to read while the player is planning a keep.
